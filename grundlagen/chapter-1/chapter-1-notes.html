GEOMETOR Commentary & Cross-Repository Notes¶

1. Hoenen on § 1 (“Wir denken drei verschiedene Systeme…”) and Erklärungen¶

Chapter I contains the three loci most intensively analyzed in geometor/hoenen (Hoenen 1954):

  • Unformalized Arithmetic in § 1 (`Caput I § 6, fn. 14`): Hoenen quotes § 1’s opening sentence verbatim in German („Wir denken drei verschiedene Systeme von Dingen…“) to point out a foundational asymmetry: while point, straight line, and plane are treated as uninterpreted symbols, cardinal and ordinal numbers (“three distinct systems”, “two points”, “at least four points”, “finite number $A_1, \ldots, A_n$”) are employed throughout Chapter I in their full intuitive arithmetic meaning.

  • Covert Existential Judgments in *Erklärungen* (`Caput IV, Prelim., fn. 1`): Notice how the 1899 German text prefixes §§ 1, 3, 4, 6, 7 with bold Erklärung (Explanation) and Definition headings (often merged or unmarked in Townsend’s English). Hoenen argues that these Erklärungen smuggle in virtual judgments of possibility and spatial structure abstracted from materia intelligibilis.

  • Linear Order (`II 1–4`), E. H. Moore (1902), and G. H. Hardy (`Caput V § 3.2.A–B, fns. 8–9`): - Edition Numbering Shift (1899 vs. 1930 7th ed.): In the 1899 Festschrift and 1902 English text, Group II has five axioms (II 1–5), where II 4 asserts that any four collinear points can be labeled $A, B, C, D$ with $B$ between $A, C$ and $A, D$, and $C$ between $A, D$ and $B, D$; Theorem 4 then orders any $n$ collinear points $A, B, C, D, E, \ldots, K$. In January 1902 (Moore 1902, cited in Townsend’s § 3 footnote!), E. H. Moore proved that II 4 is redundant—deducible from planar incidence I 1–2 and Pasch’s Axiom II 5! Consequently, in later editions (including the 1930 7th ed. cited by Hoenen), II 4 was demoted to Satz 5, shifting 1899 Satz 4 to Satz 6! - Typographic Spatial Intuition: Hoenen scrutinizes Theorem 4 (Satz 6 in 7th ed.) and Hilbert’s terms einerseits / andererseits (“on the one hand / on the other hand”) and umgekehrt (“reverse order” $K, \ldots, E, D, C, B, A$): to understand what “reverse sequence” means for $n$ points, the reader must rely on the left-to-right visual spatial order of the printed letters on the page! Hoenen crowns this critique by quoting G. H. Hardy (Mind 1929, p. 15): “If Hilbert’s axioms are to mean anything to us, we must draw figures.”

2. Euclid & Heath on §§ 6–7: Superposition, SAS (I.4), and Postulate 4 (Theorem 15)¶

Chapter I’s congruence theory (§§ 6–7) is the direct centerpiece of T. L. Heath’s commentary in geometor/euclid (Heath 1908):

  • Eliminating Superposition (`Common Notion 4`, Vol. I, pp. 228–230, folios `0242–0244`; `I.4`, pp. 249–250, folios `0263–0264`): Where Euclid proves I.4 (SAS) by “applying” (ἐφαρμόζειν) $\triangle ABC$ onto $\triangle DEF$—a move criticized by Peletier, Schopenhauer, and Bertrand Russell (Russell 1903, p. 405) as assuming rigid motion without axiom—Hilbert posits Axiom IV 6 (transferring base angles from two sides and included angle) and proves full SAS (Theorem 10) by an elegant reductio ad absurdum using uniqueness of angle transfer (IV 4). Heath reproduces both Theorem 10 (0243.g1) and Theorem 12 on supplementary angles (0244.g1) verbatim!

  • Proving Euclid’s Fourth Postulate (`Theorem 15`, Heath Vol. I, p. 200): In § 7 (Satz 15), Hilbert remarks explicitly that Euclid “erroneously, in my opinion, placed among the axioms” the theorem that all right angles are congruent (Postulate 4), and proves it purely from supplementary angles (Theorem 12) and interior ray transfer (Theorem 13)!

  • Axiom Group Swap in Later Editions: Keep in mind when consulting 20th-century commentaries that in 1899/1902 Group III = Parallels (§ 5) and Group IV = Congruence (§ 6), whereas from the 2nd/7th editions onward Hilbert swapped them so Group III = Congruence and Group IV = Parallels (matching absolute geometry’s independence from parallels).

3. The Axiom of Completeness (Vollständigkeit, § 8 Remark & Hoenen Caput V fn. 10)¶

Absent from the June 1899 German Festschrift, the Axiom of Completeness was added by Hilbert in his 1900 paper Über den Zahlbegriff and the 1900 French edition (Laugel 1900), appearing in Townsend’s 1902 English text at the close of § 8. As Heath notes on Euclid I.1 (Vol. I, p. 242, folio 0256), without Continuity circles need not intersect over arbitrary rational subfields (though Chapter VII shows they do intersect over Hilbert’s Pythagorean field $\Omega$!). And as Hoenen emphasizes (Caput V § 5, fn. 10), Vollständigkeit is openly second-order and metamathematical, quantifying over all possible extensions preserving the preceding axioms.

Cross-Repository Concordance Links¶

(See the full Euclid–Hoenen–Hilbert–Pappus Master Concordance for complete tables.)

  • § 1 (§ 1 (chapter-1)): Eliminates explicit definitions of primitive geometric objects in favor of implicit relational characterization across five axiom groups: Connection, Order, Parallels, Congruence, and Continuity. - Euclid — I.Def.1, I.Def.2, I.Def.4, I.Def.7: Replaces Euclid’s descriptive definitions (‘A point is that which has no part’, ‘A straight line lies evenly with the points on itself’) by three unspecified systems of things bound solely by relational axioms. - : Quotes Hilbert’s opening sentence verbatim in German (‘Wir denken drei verschiedene Systeme von Dingen…’) and highlights that Hilbert leaves numbers (‘two points’, ‘three planes’, real coordinates) unformalized in their intuitive sense. - : Analyzes Hilbert’s recurring heading ‘Erklärung’ (‘Definition/Explanation’) as concealing existential and intuitive judgments abstracted from spatial extension (materia intelligibilis).

  • § 2 (§ 2 (chapter-1)): Establishes the planar (I 1–2) and spatial (I 3–7) incidence axioms and proves intersection uniqueness and existence of non-collinear triples (Theorems 1–2). - Euclid — Post. 1, Post. 2, C.N. 9, XI.1–3: Formalizes both existence (I 1: Postulate 1) and uniqueness (I 2: Common Notion 9, two lines do not enclose a space) of the straight line through two points, together with planar and 3D incidence (XI.1–3) and dimensional lower bounds (I 6–7). - : Investigates how the unique determination of a straight line by two points is grasped by intuitive formal abstraction prior to axiomatic postulation. - : Planar incidence I 1–2 and spatial plane-intersection I 3–6 provide the projective incidence skeleton upon which spatial Desargues (§ 22) rests.

  • § 3, § 4 (§ 3, § 4 (chapter-1)): Formalizes ternary collinear betweenness (II 1–4) and Pasch’s planar separation axiom (II 5), deriving segment density, total linear ordering of finite point-sets, half-planes, rays, Jordan polygon separation, and spatial plane separation (Theorems 3–7). - Euclid — I.1, I.10, I.16 (tacit betweenness & planar separation): Supplies the entire linear betweenness and planar side-separation apparatus (Pasch’s Axiom II 5) absent from Euclid’s explicit postulates, blocking fallacies like the ‘all triangles are isosceles’ sophism. - : Hoenen’s centerpiece critique of pure formalism: examines II 1–4 and Theorem 4 (7th ed. Satz 5 & Satz 6: ordering n collinear points ‘A, B, C, D, E, …, K’), notes E. H. Moore’s 1902 proof that II 4 is redundant, exposes Hilbert’s tacit reliance on the visual left-to-right spatial order of typographic symbols and terms like ‘einerseits / andererseits’ and ‘umgekehrt’, and endorses G. H. Hardy’s 1929 Mind verdict: ‘If Hilbert’s axioms are to mean anything to us, we must draw figures.’

  • § 5 (§ 5 (chapter-1)): States the Euclidean Axiom of Parallels (Group III in 1899/1902; Group IV in later editions) and derives parallel transitivity (Theorem 8), preparing § 7’s alternate-angle and 180° triangle angle-sum theorems. - Euclid — Post. 5; I.27–32: Adopts Playfair’s uniqueness formulation in a given plane (at most one non-intersecting line through A to a), while existence of at least one parallel follows from exterior/alternate angle congruence (§ 7 / § 10, Euclid I.27). - : Evaluates the epistemological status of Euclidean vs. Lobachevskian/Riemannian parallel postulates with respect to intelligible extension. - : Axiom III enables Hilbert’s affine formulations of Pascal’s (Pappus’s) Theorem (§ 14, § 31) and Desargues’s Theorem (§ 22), where intersections on the line at infinity become parallel lines.

  • § 6, § 7 (§ 6, § 7 (chapter-1)): Replaces Euclidean superposition and rigid motion by six axioms of segment and angle congruence (IV 1–6), proving SAS (Theorem 10), ASA (Theorem 11), supplementary angles (Theorem 12), Euclid’s Postulate 4 on right angles (Theorem 15), SSS (Theorem 16), and the 180° triangle angle sum (Theorem 20). - Euclid — C.N. 1–4 (esp. C.N. 4 superposition): Heath (Vol. I, pp. 228–230) reproduces Hilbert’s § 6–7 congruence axioms and proofs in detail (including diagram 0243.g1 for SAS and 0244.g1 for supplementary angles) to show how Hilbert eliminates Euclid’s physical superposition (ἐφαρμόζειν). - Euclid — I.4 (SAS Triangle Congruence): Heath (Vol. I, pp. 249–250) cites Russell and Hilbert (p. 9) on why SAS cannot be proved without assuming rigid motion unless Axiom IV 6 posits base-angle transfer from two sides and the included angle, from which full SAS (Theorem 10) follows by reductio ad absurdum. - Euclid — Post. 4 (‘All right angles are equal’): Heath (Vol. I, p. 200) explicitly notes that Hilbert deduces Euclid’s Fourth Postulate as Theorem 15 from the Congruence Axioms via supplementary angles (Theorem 12). - Euclid — I.2–3, I.5, I.8, I.13, I.15, I.23, I.26, I.27, I.32: Direct deductive counterparts: I.2–3 <-> IV 1; I.26 <-> Theorem 11; I.13 <-> Theorem 12; I.5 <-> Isosceles corollary; I.8 <-> Theorem 16; I.23 <-> IV 4; I.27–29 <-> Theorem 19; I.32 <-> Theorem 20. - : Discusses congruence, rigid displacement vs. static relational axioms, and the geometric intelligibility of equality of segments and angles.

  • § 8 (§ 8 (chapter-1)): Introduces the Axiom of Archimedes (Group V) and—in the 1900/1902 Remark—the categorical Axiom of Completeness fixing the Cartesian real continuum. - Euclid — V.Def.4; X.1; I.1 & I.22: Formalizes the Eudoxus–Archimedes exhaustion principle (V.Def.4, X.1); Heath (Vol. I, p. 242 on I.1) notes that Dedekind/Hilbert Continuity is required to guarantee circle-circle and line-circle intersections, though Chapter VII (§§ 36–39) proves Euclidean ruler-and-compass intersections already exist over Hilbert’s Pythagorean field Ω without full Continuity. - : Examines Hilbert’s Axiom of Completeness (Vollständigkeitsaxiom, added 1900/1903) as a metamathematical maximality postulate over all preceding axioms, demonstrating its dependence on intuitive arithmetic and set-theoretic concepts.
