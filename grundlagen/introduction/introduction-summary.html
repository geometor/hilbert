Executive Overview¶

David Hilbert’s Grundlagen der Geometrie (The Foundations of Geometry, June 1899; authorized English translation by E. J. Townsend, 1902) opens with a celebrated epigraph from Immanuel Kant’s Kritik der reinen Vernunft (A 702 / B 730):

“All human knowledge begins with intuitions, thence passes to concepts and ends with ideas.”

(„So fängt denn alle menschliche Erkenntnis mit Anschauungen an, geht von da zu Begriffen und endigt mit Ideen.“)

In two concise paragraphs, Hilbert formulates the charter of modern axiomatics:

  1. Logical Analysis of Spatial Intuition: Just as arithmetic rests on a small number of fundamental principles, geometry requires a rigorous logical analysis of our spatial intuition—a problem pursued from Euclid down to Moritz Pasch, Giuseppe Veronese, and Felix Klein.

  2. Simplicity, Completeness, and Mutual Independence: Rather than treating axioms merely as self-evident starting points, Hilbert demands a simple and complete set of mutually independent (unabhängig) axioms partitioned into five natural groups.

  3. Architectural Scope of Individual Axiom Groups: Each major theorem of elementary and projective geometry must be traced to the minimal subset of axiom groups required for its proof—anticipating the concluding maxim of purity of method.

Townsend’s 1902 Preface¶

In the English edition, Townsend situates the treatise historically: delivered first as a winter-semester lecture course at Göttingen (1898–1899, autographed by Hans von Schaper), rearranged for the June 1899 unveiling of the Gauss–Weber Monument in Göttingen, and expanded in Léonce Laugel’s 1900 French edition with the Axiom of Completeness and Max Dehn’s polyhedral researches.
