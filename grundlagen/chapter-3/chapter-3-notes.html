GEOMETOR Commentary & Cross-Repository Notes¶

1. The Pappus–Pascal Bridge (geometor/pappus ↔ geometor/hilbert)¶

Section 14 is one of the crown jewels of the entire GEOMETOR workspace, uniting geometor/pappus, geometor/euclid, and geometor/hilbert:

  • Terminological Clarification (*Pascalscher Satz* = Pappus’s Hexagon Theorem): Following Hermann Wiener (Wiener 1891) and Friedrich Schur (Schur 1898), Hilbert calls Theorem 21 “Pascal’s Theorem” (der Pascalsche Satz) because two intersecting straight lines form a degenerate conic section in Blaise Pascal’s 1640 mystic hexagram theorem. Historically, however, this exact theorem on two straight lines was discovered and proved 1,300 years earlier by :person:`Pappus of Alexandria <pappus>` in Book VII, Propositions 138–139 of his Mathematical Collection (Synagoge, modeled in geometor/pappus!).

  • Affine Specialization: In projective space (geometor/pappus), if $A, B, C$ and $A', B', C'$ lie on two lines, the three cross-join intersections $L = CB' \cap BC'$, $M = CA' \cap AC'$, and $N = BA' \cap AB'$ are collinear. Sending the line $LM$ to the line at infinity via the Parallel Axiom (III) yields Hilbert’s affine Theorem 21:

    $$CB' \parallel BC' \;\land\; CA' \parallel AC' \implies BA' \parallel AB'.$$

  • Why Congruence Proves Pappus Without Continuity: In Euclid Book V, Eudoxus’s proportion definition (V.Def.5) invokes arbitrary integer multiples $mA \gtreqless nB$, which tacitly presupposes V.Def.4 (Archimedes’s Axiom). Hilbert bypasses Book V completely! Using only right-angle congruence and inscribed cyclic quadrilaterals (Euclid III.21–22, proved from I–IV at the end of § 7), he proves right-triangle projection operators commute ($\alpha\beta c \equiv \beta\alpha c$), which immediately forces Theorem 21, which in § 15 forces commutativity of segment multiplication $ab = ba$!

2. From Streckenrechnung to Book VI Similarity (§§ 15–17)¶

Look at the sheer elegance of §§ 15–16:

  1. René Descartes (La Géométrie, 1637) defined segment multiplication via Thales’s parallel-line proportion—assuming proportion was already known from Book V!

  2. Hilbert reverses the logical arrow: he defines $c = ab$ geometrically by the parallel construction (§ 15), proves the field laws (1–16) directly from Pappus’s Theorem (Theorem 21), defines proportion $a : b = a' : b'$ as $ab' = ba'$ (§ 16), and deduces general triangle similarity (Theorem 22, Euclid VI.4) via a stunning symmetric construction using the incenter $S$ (intersection of the three angle bisectors) and the distributive law!
